Ask which Louis Vuitton bags are most popular on the vintage and pre-owned market and the same names come up again and again: the Speedy, the Alma, the Noé, the Pochette Accessoires and the Neverfull, followed closely by the Keepall, Papillon, Saumur, Looping and the vintage Bucket. This guide compares all ten — what defines each model, who it suits, and exactly what to inspect before you buy one second-hand.

A quick note on terms: we use vintage for bags roughly twenty years old or more, and pre-owned for younger pieces. Some models below exist in both forms; others, like the Neverfull, are almost always pre-owned rather than vintage.

Louis Vuitton Speedy

The Speedy began in 1930 as a smaller companion to the house's travel bags, and the compact Speedy 25 is widely credited to a 1959 request from Audrey Hepburn. It remains the most searched-for Louis Vuitton bag on the second-hand market: light, soft-sided and unfussy, in sizes 25, 30, 35 and 40.

Buyers like it because it works with almost everything and holds far more than its silhouette suggests. Decades of production also mean healthy supply, from 1980s monogram examples to recent Damier and Epi versions.

When buying pre-owned, check the corners for rubbed canvas, the vachetta leather trim and handles for deep staining, the zipper action, and the interior for stickiness in older linings.

Louis Vuitton Alma

The Alma's domed silhouette traces back to a 1930s Gaston-Louis Vuitton design; the bag has carried the Alma name since 1992. Its structured shape, double zipper and standing base make it one of the house's most polished handbags — at home at work as much as at the weekend.

It is available in monogram and Damier canvas and in Epi leather, from the small BB to larger sizes. Epi versions shrug off rain and scratches better than vachetta-trimmed canvas.

Inspect the base corners, the condition of the rolled handles, and the zippers on both sides. On canvas versions, look closely at the vachetta base seam.

Louis Vuitton Noé

The Noé was designed in 1932 to carry five bottles of champagne — four standing, one inverted. That practical origin gave it a drawstring bucket shape that still looks current. It comes in several sizes, from the original large Noé to the Petit Noé and smaller versions.

Buyers choose it for its capacity and its relaxed, shoulder-carried ease. Vintage examples in monogram or Epi are plentiful, which keeps it comparatively attainable.

Check the drawstring and its eyelets, the base seam, and the single shoulder strap, which carries all the weight and is often the first part to show cracking on older bags.

Louis Vuitton Pochette Accessoires

Introduced in the 1990s, the Pochette Accessoires is a slim zipped pouch with a short strap — originally an accessory, now a small bag in its own right. Earlier examples are slightly smaller than current production.

It is popular as an elegant evening bag, as a first Louis Vuitton piece, and as an organiser inside larger totes. Many owners swap the short strap for a longer one to wear it crossbody.

When buying, check the zipper pull, the interior for wear marks from keys and cards, and whether the original strap is present — its absence should be reflected in the price.

Louis Vuitton Neverfull

The Neverfull launched in 2007, which means most examples on the market today are pre-owned rather than vintage under the common twenty-year definition. Its open-top tote shape, thin straps and side laces made it one of the most-carried designer bags of the past two decades, in PM, MM and GM sizes.

It appeals to commuters, parents and travellers alike: it is light, swallows a laptop, and the included pouch on later versions adds flexibility.

Inspect the strap edges — the thin straps show wear first — plus the top corners of the canvas and the interior lining. Confirm which size you are actually buying; photographs flatten the differences.

Louis Vuitton Keepall

The Keepall, the house's soft travel duffle, dates to around 1930 and remains the reference weekend bag. Sizes run from 45 to 60 centimetres; Bandoulière versions add a removable shoulder strap.

Buyers value it because it is genuinely useful: cabin-friendly in the smaller sizes, and one of the few designer purchases that earns its keep on every trip. Vintage monogram examples are widely available.

Check the handles and their attachment points, the zipper along its full length, the corners, and — on Bandoulière versions — that the original strap comes with the bag.

Louis Vuitton Papillon

The cylindrical Papillon dates to the mid-1960s and is instantly recognisable: a monogram tube with two rounded handles, often accompanied by a small matching mini-pouch. Most examples on the market today are vintage.

Its appeal is partly its shape — few bags look like it — and partly its size: the 26 and 30 centimetre versions work as compact everyday or evening bags.

Look at the seams along the curved ends, the handle wear, and whether the mini-pouch is included on versions originally sold with one. Missing pouches are common and should lower the price.

Louis Vuitton Saumur

The Saumur is a double-sided messenger bag from the 1980s, with two compartments that hang saddle-style over the shoulder — a nod to the house's equestrian heritage. It is a favourite of vintage buyers who want a hands-free, unisex bag with genuine character.

It comes in several widths, and its structure keeps its shape even when fully packed.

On older examples, examine the leather side lacing, the buckles and keeper loops, and the strap's adjustment holes, which stretch with decades of use.

Louis Vuitton Looping

The Looping, produced in the early 2000s and since discontinued, is a curved shoulder bag with a single rigid handle — a compact, distinctly Y2K silhouette that has found a new audience. Because production ended long ago, essentially every example is vintage or approaching it.

It suits buyers who want something recognisably Louis Vuitton but less ubiquitous than a Speedy, in mini, MM and GM sizes.

Check the zipper track, which curves and takes strain, the handle's attachment points, and the interior lining for wear.

Louis Vuitton Bucket

The vintage Bucket — often listed under names such as Bucket GM and PM or Marais — is an open-top monogram tote from the late 1960s with a flat base and twin shoulder straps. It has been discontinued, so examples are almost always vintage; it should not be confused with more recent drawstring designs that revisit the shape.

Buyers like its casual practicality: it carries like a tote but reads unmistakably as vintage Louis Vuitton.

The known weak point is the interior: older linings can become sticky and are frequently replaced. A professionally relined Bucket is common and acceptable — but it should be disclosed and priced accordingly. Also check the strap length for cracking.

How to choose the right model

Start with how you will actually carry the bag. For daily use, the Speedy, Alma, Noé and Neverfull are the practical core of the range. For work, the structured Alma or a larger Neverfull carry documents best. For evenings, the Pochette Accessoires and the smaller Papillon sizes are the natural picks; for travel, the Keepall stands alone. If you want something more individual, the Saumur, Looping and vintage Bucket offer character that newer bags rarely match.

Then decide between vintage and pre-owned. Vintage pieces offer discontinued shapes and older production at accessible prices; recent pre-owned pieces offer current sizing and lighter signs of use. Neither is better — they are simply different purchases.

What to check when buying vintage or pre-owned

  • Canvas and corners: cracking, rubbing or repairs at the stress points.
  • Vachetta leather: honest darkening is normal on older bags; deep stains, water marks and cracked handles are condition issues, not charm.
  • Hardware and zippers: zippers should run smoothly along their full length; check plating wear on hardware.
  • Interior: older linings can turn sticky or powdery; relining is repairable but must be disclosed.
  • Odour: smoke and mildew are difficult to remove — ask before you buy.
  • Completeness: straps, pouches, locks and keys affect value; missing pieces should show in the price.

Date codes are not authentication

From the early 1980s until March 2021, Louis Vuitton stamped most bags with a date code indicating the production period and workshop — after which the house moved to embedded microchips. A date code is useful context, but it is not proof: codes follow known formats and are easy to imitate, and a correct code can sit in a counterfeit bag.

Genuine confidence comes from examining the whole object — construction, materials, hardware, stitching, typography and provenance — which is why professional review matters more than any single stamp. You can read about how we approach this in our authentication guide.

Frequently asked questions

Which Louis Vuitton bag is the best introduction to vintage?

The Speedy. Supply is broad, prices span a wide range, and its condition issues are well understood and easy to inspect.

Is the Neverfull a vintage bag?

Not yet, in most cases. It launched in 2007, so under the common twenty-year definition nearly all examples are pre-owned rather than vintage.

Do darkened handles reduce a bag's value?

Even, honey-toned darkening of vachetta leather is expected on older bags. Uneven staining, water spots or cracking do affect value and should be reflected in the price.

Can a missing date code mean a bag is fake?

No. Very early bags predate date codes, stamps wear away, and bags made after March 2021 use microchips instead. Authentication rests on the whole bag, never one marking.

Which models suit everyday use best?

The Speedy, Noé and Neverfull for capacity; the Alma if you prefer structure; the Saumur if you want to carry hands-free.
